Level 1 · the foundations

First: what is a number?

Numbers describe amounts or positions. You already know that 3 means three things and that 8 is greater than 3. Negative numbers extend the number system to describe amounts below zero or positions to the left of zero.

Positive

Above / to the right

+4 means four steps to the right of zero. We usually leave off the plus sign and write 4.

Positive numbers are greater than zero.
Zero

The starting point

0 means no change from the starting point. It is neither positive nor negative.

Zero is the boundary between positive and negative numbers.
Negative

Below / to the left

−4 means four steps to the left of zero. The minus sign tells you which side of zero.

Negative numbers are less than zero.

Meet the number line

Every tick mark is one equal step. Moving right makes the number bigger; moving left makes it smaller. Notice that −5 is smaller than −2, even though 5 is bigger than 2. On the negative side, farther left means a smaller number.

Important distinction: the minus sign can mean two different things. In −3, it says “negative three,” a number. In 8 − 3, it says “subtract three,” an action. We'll learn to tell which job it is doing.
Level 2 · make subtraction visible

Subtraction means “start here, then move left”

For now, imagine subtraction as taking steps backward on a number line. Start at the first number. A subtraction sign means move left by the amount being subtracted. The number being subtracted tells you how many steps.

Your equation
2 − 3 = −1
Start at 2, move 3 steps left.
Start at 2 and move 3 steps left. You land on −1.
Keep the two ideas separate: “subtract” means move left. But if the amount you subtract is negative, you are subtracting a negative number. We'll explore what that means with the stories below.

What does “subtract” mean?

If you have 6 and subtract 2, you move two units left: 6 → 5 → 4. The answer is 4.

6 − 2 = 4

You can also think of it as taking away 2 from 6. The number line makes the direction visible.

Can you subtract and get a bigger answer?

Yes! If you start at 5 and subtract −2, the amount is negative. On a number line, taking away a negative amount is the opposite of taking away a positive amount: you move right 2.

5 − (−2) = 7

The next two models help explain why this makes sense.

Level 3 · temperature

Below zero is still a real temperature

A thermometer is a useful model because zero is a meaningful reference point. If the temperature is −3°C, it is three degrees below 0°C. A temperature change can go up or down.

Move the temperature

+10°0°−10°
−3°
Celsius

The temperature is 3 degrees below zero.

Temperature change challenge

It is −4°C outside. The temperature drops by 3 degrees. Where does it end?

“Drops by 3” means the temperature changes 3 degrees downward: −4 → −5 → −6 → −7.

Connect the story to the math: “It drops by 3” can be written as adding a change of −3: \(−4 + (−3)=−7\). But the question “What is −4 − (−3)?” is different: subtracting a negative change means undoing that downward change, so it goes up to −1.

Temperature subtraction: explore the difference

Suppose the temperature is −2°C now. How much warmer is 4°C than −2°C? Count the distance between the two temperatures: −2 → −1 → 0 → 1 → 2 → 3 → 4. That's 6 degrees.

4 − (−2) = 6

The answer is the distance you move from −2 to 4 when comparing them. Keep track of whether the question says “drops by” or asks you to “subtract a negative number”—they describe different actions.

Level 4 · debt and money

Negative money: owing is below zero

A balance of $0 means you owe nothing and have no money left. A balance of −$4 means you owe $4. Negative does not mean “bad”; it tells you which side of zero your balance is on.

Debt visualizer

You owe $4.

Orange coins with “−” show dollars owed. Green coins with “+” show dollars you have above zero.

What does subtracting a debt mean?

Imagine your balance is −$5: you owe $5. Then someone cancels $2 of the debt. Your debt gets smaller: −$5 becomes −$3.

−5 − (−2) = −3

“Subtract a debt of $2” means remove that $2 debt from what you owe. Since the debt is removed, your balance rises toward zero.

Think of the negative amount as something being taken away. Taking away something negative leaves you with a higher number.

A real-life note: money rules depend on the situation. This is a simplified balance model to show the math, not financial advice.

Level 5 · the elevator

Floors below ground are negative numbers

Imagine a building with a lobby on floor 0, floors 1, 2, and 3 above it, and basement floors −1, −2, and −3 below it. The floor number tells you where you are. Going up makes the number bigger; going down makes it smaller.

Ride the elevator

Choose a starting floor and a movement. Watch where the elevator lands.

Your elevator trip
−2 − 3 = −5

Start at −2 and go down 3 floors to −5.

Remember: floor 0 is the lobby. Basement floors are negative. A floor number is a location, while “go up” and “go down” describe movement.

What does subtracting a negative floor change mean?

You are on floor −2. A planned elevator movement of −3 floors means going down 3 floors. If you subtract that movement, you undo the downward trip: the elevator goes up 3 floors instead.

−2 − (−3) = 1

Start at basement floor −2. Subtracting the negative movement reverses it, so go up: −1, 0, 1.

Compare these carefully:

Go down 3 floors from −2
−2 + (−3) = −5
Subtract a downward movement of −3
−2 − (−3) = 1

This model is about floor numbers and movement. The elevator is not literally “taking away a floor”; it helps you picture reversing a negative change.

Level 5 · why the rule works

Why does subtracting a negative become addition?

You may hear “two negatives make a positive.” That phrase alone can be confusing. Instead, use the models to understand the action. Then the shortcut becomes a summary of what you already understand.

Model 1 · number line

Subtract means reverse the direction of the amount

For 5 − 2, start at 5 and move 2 steps left: you land at 3. For 5 − (−2), you are subtracting an amount that points left. Reversing that direction means move 2 steps right: you land at 7.

Subtract positive 2
5 − 2 = 3

Move left 2.

Subtract negative 2
5 − (−2) = 7

Move right 2.

Model 2 · debt

Taking away an owing amount

You owe $5, so your balance is −5. If $2 of debt disappears, you owe only $3. Your balance improves from −5 to −3.

−5 − (−2) = −3

Removing debt moves your balance to the right, toward zero.

Model 3 · opposite operations

Subtraction undoes addition

Adding −2 moves you 2 to the left. Subtracting −2 undoes that leftward change, so it moves you 2 to the right. These operations cancel each other.

x − (−2) = x + 2

For example, 5 − (−2) = 5 + 2 = 7.

The rule, now that you know why: subtracting a number is the same as adding its opposite. The opposite of −2 is +2, so 5 − (−2) = 5 + 2. The opposite of +2 is −2, so 5 − 2 = 5 + (−2).

Turn subtraction into addition

Use the helper to see the transformation.

Same value, two forms
−3 − (−4) = 1
−3 + 4 = 1

Subtracting −4 is the same as adding its opposite, +4.

Level 6 · avoid the traps

Three common mistakes—and how to fix them

“A negative is always smaller in absolute size.”

Not quite. Compare the actual positions: −8 is less than −3 because −8 is farther left. A number's value is its position, not just the size of the digits.

−8 < −3

“Two minus signs always make a plus.”

Don't blindly count symbols. In −5 − (−2), the first minus belongs to negative five; the second is the subtraction action; the last belongs to negative two. Think “subtract the opposite” to transform the operation.

−5 − (−2) = −5 + 2

“Subtracting always makes smaller.”

Subtracting a positive number moves left, but subtracting a negative number moves right. The operation and the sign of the amount work together.

4 − 6 = −2
4 − (−6) = 10

For tutors, families, and curious learners

How this learning path is designed

Move from meaning to symbols

The sequence begins with concrete stories (temperature and debt), moves to a visual number line, and then connects the models to equations and the shortcut. Students are asked to explain, explore, and practice rather than only memorize.

Suggested routine: 10–15 minutes per visit. Work through one section, say the reasoning aloud, answer a few questions, then revisit a previous section on another day. Avoid racing through all levels in one sitting.

Evidence-informed choices

The U.S. What Works Clearinghouse practice guide recommends systematic instruction, clear mathematical language, visual representations, and number lines for students who struggle with mathematics. This site uses those approaches. Short practice with immediate explanations is included, but a website is a supplement—not a substitute for teacher feedback and individualized support.

No single technique guarantees learning for every child. Check understanding by asking students to explain their reasoning and solve new problems.

Research and free professional resources

  • Institute of Education Sciences / What Works Clearinghouse — Assisting Students Struggling with Mathematics: Intervention in the Elementary Grades (2021). Evidence-based recommendations include systematic instruction, mathematical language, representations, and number lines.
  • Regional Educational Laboratory — Number line and representation professional learning resources. Explains how number lines help students compare quantities, model operations, and prepare for later math.
  • Retrieval Practice in Stepwise Worked Examples Improves Learning (2025). A study with adult learners found that prompts to retrieve each upcoming step improved later recall and problem-solving compared with studying examples without those prompts. This supports the site's “try, get feedback, explain” design, but it is not direct proof of this exact website's effectiveness with middle-school students.

Accuracy note: these resources inform the instructional design; this exact site has not been experimentally tested. The visual models are aids for understanding, and each real-world story has limits.
